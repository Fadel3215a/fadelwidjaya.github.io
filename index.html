Research & Thinking

Practical research and decision-making shaped by project coordination, stakeholder perspectives, operational constraints, and continuous improvement.

Research

During the LRT Jakarta project, I often had to research operational problems before supporting a next step. When coordinating Material Sample Approval (MSA) documentation, I identified that some documents remained in NONO-C status and could not progress to Stage 3. Before escalating, I reviewed the outstanding requirements and MSA checklist, then discussed the issue with the Engineer, Chief Engineer, and Site Engineer Manager to understand exactly what was still preventing closure.

In other coordination work — such as gathering vendor information — I applied the same approach: gather information from available sources, consult relevant teams, and clarify requirements before supporting a direction.

Strategic judgment

When MSA documents could not progress because outstanding comments and approvals were unresolved, I did not escalate externally without internal verification first. The sequence was: identify the blocker → review requirements → discuss with the Engineer → confirm with senior engineering personnel → determine appropriate escalation → prepare follow-up to PMC → coordinate approvals → verify closure.

When evaluating vendors or other operational options, I applied the same discipline: consider reliability, capability, responsiveness, and team feedback before supporting the most practical direction — without assuming the strongest option on paper was automatically the right one.

Perspective comparison

Comparing internal teams, external stakeholders, vendors, and other sources before supporting an operational decision.

In project coordination, different stakeholders often provide different perspectives on the same problem. Internal teams may understand project requirements more deeply, while external teams and vendors may provide practical information about available solutions. I compare these perspectives instead of relying on a single opinion. On the LRT Jakarta project, this type of coordination involved gathering and comparing information from vendors such as Schneider, R2P, and Huawei, as well as external stakeholders including JAKPRO, PMC OCG-OJD Joint Operation and Association, Waskita, and Nindya — not always in the same decision, but across the broader coordination work.

The MSA closure process showed a different kind of perspective comparison: the Engineer, Chief Engineer, Site Engineer Manager, contractor, PMC, and other project personnel each had distinct responsibilities in the approval workflow. Understanding those roles helped me coordinate follow-up and gather the correct documentation without treating every party as part of the same decision.

Rejected ideas

I have encountered situations where a recommended vendor or solution appeared suitable initially but was not adopted because it did not fully match the project requirements. Some options had limitations that became clearer after further discussion or implementation. Rather than forcing the original choice, I supported the evaluation of alternatives and adjusted the approach when available information showed that another option was more suitable.

Weakness analysis

MSA document control showed me how unresolved comments, missing approvals, incomplete documentation, or unclear ownership can block project progression — even when a document appears close to completion. A document in NONO-C status could not move to Stage 3 until every dependency was closed, which was not always visible at first glance.

Operational decisions more broadly can be affected by incomplete information, assumptions, or changing project conditions. Feedback from implementation — or from attempting to advance a document to the next stage — often reveals problems that were not visible during initial review.

Framework development

Experiences like MSA coordination contributed to how I think about structured problem solving: problem identified → information gathered → relevant perspectives consulted → action determined → action implemented → requirements verified → issue closed → next stage enabled. I began to see this pattern across operational work, not only in document control.

This way of thinking later influenced my development of the Continuous Research & Development Loop (CRDL) — my original framework developed through my research and experience, not an established industry methodology. CRDL extends a similar continuous learning principle into strategic research and content strategy.

Continuous improvement

The MSA closure process showed me that completing an action is not the same as closing an issue. I had to identify the blocker, understand what was causing it, coordinate the relevant parties, follow up on outstanding comments and signatures, verify completion, and only then could the document move from NONO-C to fully closed NONO status and proceed to Stage 3.

Vendor coordination taught a similar lesson: implementation often surfaces new problems. Each completed step should generate information that informs the next decision — whether that means adjusting an approach, escalating a follow-up, or confirming that requirements are truly closed.

Operational coordination shaped how I approach research and decision-making. See the MSA documentation closure case study for a detailed example. The LinkedIn Organic Growth Playbook and CRDL framework extend this thinking into strategic research, experimentation, and continuous improvement.
